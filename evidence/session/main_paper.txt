Abstract
We analyze short-horizon directional predictability across eight CME futures instruments us-
ing 6.83 billion Market-by-Order events from 2025. A pre-registered methodology produces
10,464 model fits across 46 instrument-session cells with Bonferroni-corrected significance test-
ing. Three findings emerge. First, the pre-registered null hypothesis of no economic exploitability
was upheld, but all 46 cells exhibit statistically significant directional predictability replicating
across three holdout designs (Spearman ρ > 0.97). Second, predictability varies 40-fold across
cells and is associated with matching algorithm design: K-Algorithm instruments show 8 .3×
the predictability of FIFO instruments, mediated by a bounce rate mechanism ( ρ = +0.94), and
overnight sessions show 4.0× the predictability of regular trading hours. Third, the exploitability
boundary is set by spread-direction arithmetic: 0 /46 cells profit at retail costs, 46 /46 at market-
maker costs. Simple academic signals capture a median 89.5% of ML edge, confirming that
the ML apparatus primarily validates known order book mechanics. A four-mechanism leakage
taxonomy and comparison to canonical microstructure signals are detailed in the paper.
Keywords: Market microstructure, price predictability, high-frequency trading, matching algo-
rithms, order book, CME Globex, machine learning, pre-registration

1. Introduction
The efficiency of modern electronic futures markets has been extensively studied through the lens
of high-frequency trading participation and its effects on price discovery, liquidity provision, and
market quality (Hendershott, Jones & Menkveld, 2011; Brogaard, Hendershott & Riordan, 2014;
Menkveld, 2013). A central finding of this literature is that algorithmic trading improves informa-
tional efficiency by reducing arbitrage opportunities and accelerating price adjustment. However,
the relationship between HFT participation and residual price predictability has been characterized
primarily through cross-sectional venue comparisons or natural experiments at the daily level.
This paper contributes a substantially finer-grained measurement. Using 6.83 billion Market-
by-Order events from CME Globex across eight futures instruments — equity indices (ES, NQ),
energy (CL), metals (GC), agricultural grains (ZC, ZS), and livestock (HE, LE) — we construct
46 instrument-session cells that partition each instrument’s trading day into time-of-day buckets
ranging from the Asia overnight session to the US afternoon close. Within each cell, we train six
machine learning architectures at four prediction horizons under a pre-registered three-design hold-
out methodology, producing 10,464 individual model fits. The granularity of this approach enables
us to measure how predictability varies not just across instruments but across sessions within the
same instrument.
1.1 Principal Findings
Our findings are organized around three contributions to the market microstructure literature.
Contribution 1: Ubiquitous but heterogeneous predictability. Directional predictability at
the 5-to-60-second horizon exists in all 46 instrument-session cells. This finding replicates across
three independent holdout designs — stratified block (Design A), alternative stratification (Design
B), and walk-forward (Design C) — with rank correlation exceeding 0.97 across all design pairs. No
cell shows negative edge under any design. The finding is robust to feature ablation: no single feature
carries more than 5% of the total signal, and features with theoretical lookahead risk contribute less
than 1%.
The magnitude of predictability varies by a factor of 40 across cells. The strongest cells (corn
futures overnight, AUC = 0.873) exhibit edge-over-chance of +0.361, while the weakest (NQ Nasdaq
futures across all sessions) hover at +0.010-0.020, barely above the noise floor. This heterogeneity
is itself the primary finding: it is not random but systematically determined by market structure
parameters.
Contribution 2: Market structure determinants. Three factors are associated with cross-cell
variation in predictability. The two K-Algorithm instruments (corn, soybeans) show median edge
8.3 times stronger than the six FIFO instruments. This is consistent with a matching-algorithm
effect operating through the bounce rate mechanism — K-Algorithm instruments exhibit 70% mid-
price reversal probability versus 56% for FIFO (ρ = +0.94 between bounce rate and predictability).
However, with only two K-Algorithm instruments in the sample, instrument-level confounds (grain-
specific market structure, lower HFT participation) cannot be ruled out. Across all 46 cells, HFT

participation intensity (proxied by cancel-to-trade ratio) correlates negatively with predictability ( ρ
= −0.65, p < 0.001). Within FIFO instruments alone, the correlation is ρ = −0.61, providing the
cleaner within-category test of the HFT-efficiency hypothesis (§7.3). Session structure provides the
third dimension: overnight sessions with reduced HFT participation show 4.0 times the predictability
of regular trading hours across all applicable instruments.
Contribution 3: The predictability-exploitability gap.Under a three-scenario execution model,
no cell produces positive expected value at retail costs ($2.50/side + full spread) — even the strongest
(ZC overnight, 86% directional accuracy) yields −$16.14 per trade (see §6.2 worked example).
At market-maker costs ($0.10/side, earning the spread), all 46 cells become profitable, with NQ
overnight paradoxically generating the highest dollar profit ($45/attempt) despite having the weak-
est statistical signal, due to FIFO fill priority and high notional value. The sharp 0/46 → 46/46
transition is largely driven by the arithmetic of spread-direction reversal rather than a subtle struc-
tural mechanism, but the decomposition of fee effects versus spread effects is itself informative for
market design.
1.2 Additional Contributions
Beyond the three principal findings, we make several methodological and empirical contributions.
We establish a four-mechanism leakage taxonomy specific to second-resolution MBO futures
research. Each mechanism operates through a distinct pathway: bid-ask bounce conflation (Mecha-
nism 1), Level 3 MBO contemporaneous leakage (Mechanism 2, producing 23-fold apparent signal
inflation), Level 2 market structure reflection (Mechanism 3), and Level 1 trade flow contamination
(Mechanism 4). The taxonomy provides a practical checklist for researchers working with high-
frequency order book data.
We test four canonical academic signals — Order Flow Imbalance (Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov,
2014), VPIN (Easley, López de Prado & O’Hara, 2012), Order Flow Acceleration, and Depth Asym-
metry Ratio — per session bucket. DAR is algebraically identical to the l1_imbalance feature already
present in our ML baseline, making the simple-vs-ML comparison a test of three genuinely new sig-
nals (OFI, VPIN, OFA) plus one redundant signal (DAR). Simple logistic regression using all four
captures a median 89.5% of the ML edge (Spearman ρ = +0.97, n = 43 cells), indicating that com-
plex models primarily rediscover known order book mechanics. ML adds genuine value specifically
on high-volume FIFO overnight cells (ES Europe overnight: simple +0.056 vs. ML +0.182), where
non-linear feature interactions provide additional predictive power.
We test 320 alternative label configurations spanning five label types (L1 through L5) to ensure
findings are not specific to binary direction prediction. Persistence labels (L4a) show genuine signal
in 24 of 32 instrument-horizon configurations — real directional content that persists for 3-5 seconds
after order book events — but this signal remains economically unexploitable after transaction costs.
We conduct 192 data depth ablation experiments establishing the marginal value of Level 2
and Level 3 order book data. After correcting for the contemporaneous leakage mechanism, Level 3
MBO features add only +0.0015 AUC on average beyond Level 2, establishing that MBO data carries
near-zero marginal predictive value at one-second resolution under correct causal construction.

Finally, pooled analysis with bucket-indicator features (3,840 additional runs) confirms that di-

outperform pooled by 0.007-0.118 edge.
1.3 Related Work and Positioning
This paper relates to four literatures. To the HFT-efficiency literature (Hendershott et al., 2011; Bro-
gaard et al., 2014; Menkveld, 2013; Budish, Cramton & Shim, 2015), we provide the first contin-
uous measurement of the HFT-predictability relationship across instruments and intraday sessions,
demonstrating that efficiency varies smoothly with participation intensity. To the matching algorithm
design literature (Biais, Bisière & Spatt, 2010; Yao & Ye, 2018; Haynes & Onur, 2020), we provide
direct evidence linking algorithm choice to informational efficiency through the bounce rate mech-
anism. To the ML-for-LOB literature (Gu, Kelly & Xiu, 2020; Zhang, Zohren & Roberts, 2019;
Sirignano & Cont, 2019; Briola, Bartolucci & Aste, 2025), we demonstrate that simple signals cap-
ture the overwhelming majority of ML edge, and that the pre-registration methodology with three
holdout designs provides a template for rigorous signal validation. To the replication crisis literature
(Harvey, Liu & Zhu, 2016; McLean & Pontiff, 2016; López de Prado, 2018), we demonstrate a
pre-registered, pre-committed analytical framework that documents five deviations from the original
plan with explicit justification.

tail. Section 3 describes the data, instruments, session bucket construction, and pipeline verification

the main empirical results across all 46 cells and three designs. Section 6 presents the economic

market structure mechanisms — matching algorithm effects, bounce rate mediation, HFT proxy

2. Related Literature
2.1 Market Microstructure Theory: Foundations
The theoretical framework for short-horizon price prediction rests on foundational contributions.
Kyle (1985) establishes the continuous-time model of strategic informed trading, deriving the lin-
ear price impact function λ that relates signed order flow to permanent price changes. Glosten and
Milgrom (1985) model the bid-ask spread as an adverse selection premium, establishing that market
maker quotes fully incorporate informational content of order flow in equilibrium — implying that
4

===== PAGE 6 =====
once quotes adjust, the predictive content of past order flow is exhausted. At one-second resolu-
tion with the universal lag we impose, the Glosten-Milgrom mechanism predicts that only residual
inefficiencies — those not yet arbitraged by faster participants — should remain exploitable.
Roll (1984) provides the most directly relevant tool for our artifact verification framework. Roll’s
estimator for the effective bid-ask spread from the serial covariance of price changes identifies the
mechanical bounce phenomenon: successive prices oscillate between bid and ask around the efficient
price, producing negative serial correlation that carries no directional information. Our bounce-free
(BFree) feature construction is a direct implementation of Roll’s insight.
Hasbrouck (1991a, 1991b) extends the information decomposition to permanent and transitory
price impact components. The transitory component — reflecting market frictions including bid-ask
bounce and inventory effects — is precisely what our BFree specification removes. Cont, Kukanov,
and Stoikov (2014) introduce Order Flow Imbalance as a linear predictor of same-second price
changes. The critical distinction in our analysis is between their contemporaneous measurement
(OFI at time t predicting returns at time t) and lagged prediction (OFI at t−1 predicting returns at t).
2.2 High-Frequency Trading and Market Efficiency
Hendershott, Jones, and Menkveld (2011) document that algorithmic trading narrows spreads and
improves informational efficiency, with quote revisions occurring within milliseconds of informa-
tion arrival. Menkveld (2013) characterizes HFT firms as dominant liquidity providers maintaining
inventory for seconds. Brogaard, Hendershott, and Riordan (2014) show that HFT firms participate
in 73% of NASDAQ trades and contribute positively to permanent price changes. Budish, Cramton,
and Shim (2015) formalize the latency race as a Nash equilibrium where trading profits are competed
away through hardware investment.
Our contribution extends this literature by measuring the efficiency-predictability relationship
continuously across sessions and instruments, rather than through cross-sectional venue compar-
isons. The intraday variation in HFT participation within a single instrument provides a within-
instrument control. Our finding that cancel-to-trade ratio correlates with predictability at ρ = −0.65
across 46 cells (compared to an earlier full-session analysis findingρ = −0.714 across 8 instruments)
strengthens the HFT-efficiency mechanism at substantially greater statistical power.
2.3 Matching Algorithm Design
CME Globex operates multiple matching algorithms: pure price-time FIFO for equity index and
energy futures, and Split FIFO/Pro-Rata (the “K-Algorithm”) for CBOT grain products. Under K-
Algorithm matching, approximately 40% of volume at a price level is allocated by time priority and
60% pro-rata by size, creating incentives for concentrated resting depth.
Haynes and Onur (2020) study the May 2015 switch from pro-rata to FIFO in CME 2-year
Treasury futures, finding that prices are less efficient under pro-rata due to higher reversal events.
Field and Large (2008) model pro-rata matching in one-tick markets. Aspris, Foley, Harris, and
O’Neill (2015) study time pro-rata in LIFFE STIR futures. Our contribution is the first empirical
5

===== PAGE 7 =====
documentation of the K-Algorithm hybrid’s distinctively high bounce dynamics — 70% reversal rate
compared to 56% for pure FIFO — and its mediation of the matching algorithm → predictability
relationship.
2.4 Machine Learning for Limit Order Books
Zhang, Zohren, and Roberts (2019) introduce DeepLOB, combining CNN and LSTM architectures
for LOB prediction. Sirignano and Cont (2019) find universal price formation features across 489
equities. Ntakaris et al. (2018) introduce the FI-2010 benchmark. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) provide
the most comprehensive ML comparison for equity returns. Shwartz-Ziv and Armon (2022) show
XGBoost outperforms deep learning on tabular data benchmarks.
Briola, Bartolucci, and Aste (2025) demonstrate that high LOB forecasting power does not yield
actionable trading signals once realistic execution is imposed — a finding directly convergent with
ours, obtained independently on different asset classes. Our analysis extends beyond prior work in
four respects: MBO-level data rather than aggregated snapshots, pre-registered three-design holdout,
systematic variation across 46 instrument-session cells, and direct comparison between ML and
simple academic signals.
2.5 Pre-Registration and Multiple Testing
Harvey, Liu, and Zhu (2016) propose t-statistic thresholds of 3.0 for new factors given the multiple
testing conducted across the literature. López de Prado (2018) advocates combinatorial purged cross-
validation and probability of backtest overfitting metrics. Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado, and Zhu
(2014) formalize the multiple testing problem. McLean and Pontiff (2016) document 26% out-of-
sample and 58% post-publication decay of financial anomalies.
Our pre-registration approach addresses these concerns directly. The complete analytical proto-
col was committed to version control before any model training, with five documented deviations.
The three-design holdout tests whether findings are sensitive to holdout selection: Designs A and
B vary the seed (testing block-selection sensitivity), while Design C varies the temporal structure
(testing walk-forward stability). The cross-design Spearman of 0.97+ establishes within-sample
stability across holdout configurations, though it does not substitute for a genuine forward test on
out-of-sample data.

3.2 Session Bucket Construction
We partition each instrument’s trading day into time-of-day buckets. All boundaries are defined in
New York local time with DST transitions handled programmatically.
Default scheme (ES, NQ, CL, GC) — 7 buckets: Asia overnight (18:00 prior – 02:00 NY , ~8
hrs), Europe overnight (02:00 – 08:30, ~6.5 hrs), Open (08:30 – 10:00, 1.5 hrs), Morning (10:00 –
12:00, 2 hrs), Midday (12:00 – 14:00, 2 hrs), Afternoon close (14:00 – 16:15, 2.25 hrs), Full session
(18:00 prior – 16:15, ~22.25 hrs).
ZC/ZS adapted scheme — 5 buckets:Overnight (20:00 prior – 08:45, ~12.75 hrs), Open (09:30
– 10:30, 1 hr), Morning (10:30 – 12:30, 2 hrs), Afternoon (12:30 – 14:20, ~1.83 hrs), Full session
(20:00 prior – 14:20, ~18.33 hrs).
HE/LE adapted scheme — 4 buckets: Open (09:30 – 10:30, 1 hr), Morning (10:30 – 12:30, 2
hrs), Afternoon (12:30 – 14:05, ~1.58 hrs), Full session (09:30 – 14:05, ~4.58 hrs).
Total instrument-bucket cells: 4 × 7 + 2 × 5 + 2 × 4 = 46.
The full session bucket for each instrument provides a meta-comparison: does bucket-specific
training improve over full-session training? If overnight-specific models outperform full-session
models, the signal is session-dependent.
3.3 Data Processing Pipeline
Raw MBO events are processed through a three-stage pipeline. Stage 1 (mbo_loader.py) loads com-
pressed .dbn.zst files with streaming memory management and instrument-specific session filters.

Stage 2 (book_engine.py) reconstructs the limit order book at 1-second intervals using cold-start re-
construction per trading day, producing snapshot parquets with top-5 bid/ask price and depth. Stage
3 (flow_tagger.py) classifies trades using exchange-assigned aggressor flags, eliminating tick-rule
classification error.
3.4 Data Quality Discoveries and Corrections
Five deviations from the pre-registered protocol were documented. We describe the three most
methodologically significant in detail because each illustrates a class of data quality issue relevant
to researchers working with CME MBO data.
3.4.1 Databento Delivery Completeness Divergence (Deviation 4)
Verification of ES reprocessing revealed a systematic 3.88% divergence in trade event counts
between two download methods (15-minute chunks vs. full-session). Diagnostic analysis traced the
divergence to a Databento delivery mechanism: the chunk method captured aggressor-side trade
events whose fill-event counterparts existed in the full-session download but whose trade events did
not. The arithmetic identity was exact: 207,455 contracts of excess in chunk downloads equaled
207,455 contracts of fill orphans in full-session downloads. All 50 sampled orphan fills had match-
ing trade events at the exact same nanosecond timestamp with canonical flags and channel IDs,
confirming they were real events missing from the full-session delivery.
The 15-minute chunk method was adopted as canonical. This discovery has practical implica-
tions for any researcher using Databento’s CME MBO data: the download method affects complete-
ness by approximately 4% for trade events, which can bias flow-based features.
3.4.2 Stale-Order Gap-Fill Discovery (Deviation 5)
Book reconstruction diagnostics on NQ, ZC, and ZS identified negative bid-ask spreads persist-
ing across multiple consecutive snapshots — a physical impossibility in a functioning limit order
book. Root-cause analysis traced the artifact to CME’s daily maintenance window (16:00-17:00
CT) and CBOT’s grain pre-open break (07:45-08:30 CT). Orders placed just before these bound-
aries had cancellation events falling within unpurchased data gaps. The stale orders lingered in the
reconstructed book, crossing the opposite side when the market reopened and moved.
Severity varied across the three materially affected instruments: NQ (43% of overnight snap-
shots showed negative spreads), ZS (80%), and ZC (16%). Gap-fill data was purchased for all six
instruments with overnight sessions (ES, NQ, CL, GC, ZC, ZS; ~1,500 files, minimal cost) to ensure
completeness across the CME maintenance window. Because the affected time window could not
be identified in advance without inspecting each instrument’s book reconstruction, we purchased
broadly to ensure consistent data treatment across all overnight-session instruments. Only NQ, ZS,
and ZC showed material negative spread artifacts. ES, GC, and CL had negligible pre-fix negative
spread rates (<1%) because their overnight sessions do not span the CBOT grain pre-open break that
produced the most severe stale orders. After correction, negative spread rates on the three affected
instruments fell to 0.3-3%, with residual cases handled by a negative-spread mask (features derived
from mid-price set to NaN when bid > ask).
This discovery illustrates a general vulnerability of MBO book reconstruction: any gap in the

event stream produces stale orders that propagate forward until corrected. Researchers using MBO
data from any source should verify spread positivity as a first diagnostic.
3.4.3 Convention Z Parser Fix (Deviation 3)
Initial raw data verification identified a filename-convention ambiguity. All eight instruments
use Convention Z (filename encodes the New York trading day), correcting an initial assessment
that attributed different conventions to different instruments. A tangential discovery during this
investigation: January 9, 2025 was a half-trading-day due to the national Day of Mourning for
President Carter, requiring bucket-level exclusion where the truncated session covered less than 50%
of normal bucket duration.
3.4.4 Half-Session Exclusion (Deviation 1)
January 9, 2025 was a CME-designated early close for the national Day of Mourning for Pres-
ident Jimmy Carter. For buckets where the venue close falls inside the bucket window with less
than 50% of normal duration remaining, the bucket observation is excluded for that day. This af-
fects afternoon-close buckets on the default-scheme instruments. One trading day is removed from
affected buckets; no impact on cross-design agreement.
3.4.5 ZC/ZS Overnight Boundary Amendment (Deviation 2)
The pre-registered overnight bucket boundaries for ZC/ZS were specified as 19:00-09:30 NY .
Verification against actual CBOT venue hours revealed the correct boundaries are 20:00-08:45 NY
for the electronic session. The bucket specification was amended to match actual trading hours. This
corrects a specification error in the pre-registration; the methodology (train on actual trading data
within the bucket) is unchanged.
3.5 Verification Protocol
Before model training, a four-level verification protocol was applied per the pre-registration (Section
7).
V1 (Schema consistency): After ES reprocessing, new parquets were verified to have identical
column names and dtypes to existing processed parquets. Result: PASS on all 12 months.
V2 (Numerical consistency): New ES parquets filtered to 09:30-11:30 NY were compared
against existing parquets. Row counts matched within 0.03% (below the 0.1% tolerance). Naive
AUC matched within 0.00003 (below the 0.0001 tolerance). Result: PASS.
V3 (Headline replication): A single baseline run (LightGBM, BFree, 5s horizon) on June 2025
ES data filtered to 09:30-11:30 NY was compared against prior locked baseline values. BFree AUC
matched within 0.003 (below the 0.005 tolerance). Result: PASS.
V4 (Cross-instrument sanity): The pre-registered sanity check requires that cancel-to-trade
ratio rank order across buckets follows the expected pattern (Open > Morning > Afternoon-close
> Midday > Europe overnight > Asia overnight) on at least 6 of 8 applicable instruments. Result:
PASS on 7 of 8 instruments (GC showed a minor inversion between Midday and Afternoon-close
ranks, within the tolerance of 2 permitted failures). Daily volume on the full-session bucket was
verified against CME published data within the 5% tolerance for all 8 instruments.

3.6 Feature Construction
From the 1-second order book snapshots, we construct two feature sets.
The Full feature set contains 15 features spanning three data tiers. Level 1 features: spread
(ticks), best bid and ask depth, Level 1 order book imbalance (bid_size − ask_size) / (bid_size +
ask_size), and mid-price returns at 1, 5, 10, and 30 seconds. Level 2 features: total depth across 5
levels for each side, depth-weighted imbalance, 300-second depth percentile, and 30-second depth
change. Level 3 features: cancel-to-trade ratio at 10-second and 60-second windows, and minutes
since session open.
The BFree (bounce-free) feature set removes the four mid-price return features. The Full-BFree
comparison isolates whether prediction derives from return autocorrelation (bounce) versus order
book structural features (genuine signal). An earlier full-session analysis established that the Full-
BFree gap on ZC is 28.2 percentage points (Full AUC 0.957, BFree AUC 0.675), confirming that the
overwhelming majority of apparent predictability on K-Algorithm instruments reflects mechanical
bounce.
All features are subject to a mandatory causal lag: features at time t use information available
only through t −1 (implemented as shift(1)). The prediction target is binary mid-price direction at
four horizons: 5, 10, 30, and 60 seconds. Zero-tick observations (no mid-price change) are excluded
from training and evaluation.
4. Methodology
4.1 Pre-Registration
The complete analytical protocol was committed to git version control before any model training
began (commit 9c372857 , dated 2026-04-15 08:05:36 PT). The repository is publicly archived at
https://github.com/TheoJHoward/cme-futures-microstructure , where the commit hash,
contents, and full subsequent history can be inspected. The pre-registration document is included in
the repository as MASTER_FINDINGS/preregistration_v4.txt . The first model training output
for the v4 protocol is dated 2026-04-19, four days after the pre-registration commit.
The pre-registration specifies: the primary and secondary research questions with directional pre-
dictions, all eight instruments and their data scope (January-December 2025), all bucket definitions
with exact NY-time boundaries, three holdout designs with pre-committed random seeds (SEED_A =
1,137,580,267, SEED_B = 1,820,754,789, SEED_C = 1,943,184,771), six model architectures with
fixed hyperparameters, four prediction horizons, two feature sets, the Bonferroni correction thresh-
old, the five-criterion cross-design agreement rule (Section 8 of the pre-registration), the four-level
verification protocol, and the 14-phase execution sequence (A0-A14).
The pre-registered primary prediction was that no instrument-bucket cell would satisfy all five
confirmation criteria — including criterion 5, the transaction cost threshold. This prediction was
upheld. Five deviations from the pre-registration were documented during execution (see §3.4 and
Internet Appendix IA.10), none affecting holdout designs, significance criteria, or the cross-design

agreement rule. All deviations were documented at the time they were made, following the protocol

4.2 Three Holdout Designs
Design A (Stratified block holdout):Six blocks of 10 trading days each, drawn with a no-adjacency
constraint (no blocks from adjacent months), stratified by half-month. Five-day embargo on each
side. Random seed: 1,137,580,267.
Design B (Alternative stratification): Same structure, different random seed 1,820,754,789,
with the constraint that no Design B block shares more than 5 trading days with any Design A block.
Design C (Walk-forward): Eight monthly rolling folds. Fold k trains on months 1 through k+3
and tests on month k+4, with 5-day embargo. This design tests temporal stability without relying on
random block selection.
4.3 Model Architectures
Six architectures span the complexity spectrum. All hyperparameters are fixed across all instrument-
horizon-bucket combinations to prevent ex-post optimization. This design choice is methodologi-
cally conservative — particularly for deep learning models, which typically benefit from per-task
tuning (Shwartz-Ziv & Armon, 2022) — but eliminates the risk that reported performance reflects
hyperparameter search rather than genuine signal. The DL architecture results should be inter-
preted under this constraint: the negative DL advantage we report may partly reflect the fixed-
hyperparameter protocol rather than a categorical architectural inferiority.

Tree models use the validation set for monitoring but train to the full 500 estimators. The test set
comprises the holdout blocks (Designs A/B) or the test fold (Design C).
Deep learning sequence construction. LSTM, CNN, and Transformer models use sequence
inputs of length 10 seconds: snapshots from t −9 through t −1, all within the causal window. Se-
quences that would cross session boundaries (market open/close) or contract roll dates are excluded
via sentinel masking — the 10-second window must fall entirely within a single continuous trading
session. This prevents information leakage across sessions and eliminates the possibility of artificial
discontinuities contaminating learned representations.
Bit-exact replication. Deterministic training (n_jobs=1 for trees, fixed CUDA seeds for DL)
was verified through a pilot protocol: the same LightGBM model trained on identical data on both
compute nodes (Node A: AMD Ryzen/RTX 4080; Compute Node B: Intel i7-12700/RTX 4080)
produced bit-exact outputs (identical test AUC to 15 decimal places). DL models were verified to
produce consistent results within floating-point tolerance across the two GPUs. During the study,
a PyTorch installation error on Node B (CPU-only build installed instead of CUDA-enabled) was
discovered and corrected after approximately 6 days of compute; all DL results produced after the
correction used GPU acceleration identical to Node A.
Each cell × design produces 48 runs: 6 architectures × 4 horizons × 2 feature sets. Total
isolation: 46 × 48 × 3 = 6,624 runs.
4.4 The Four-Mechanism Leakage Taxonomy
Through the artifact verification process (developed during an earlier phase of this research), we
identify four distinct forms of lookahead bias specific to second-resolution MBO futures research.
We present these as a practical checklist: any researcher working with high-frequency order book
data should verify that their feature construction does not suffer from these mechanisms. We are not
aware of a prior published study using CME MBO data that explicitly documents applying all four
corrections. We cannot determine which published results might be affected without access to exact
feature construction code, but the 23-fold mean inflation from Mechanism 2 alone suggests that any
study reporting large AUC gains from MBO features at one-second resolution without documenting
shift(1) application should be interpreted with caution.
Mechanism 1 (Bid-ask bounce conflation): Price-lagged return features encode mechanical
bounce (Roll, 1984). Models trained with these features learn to predict reversal rather than genuine
12

===== PAGE 14 =====
direction. Correction: BFree feature set removing all price-lagged returns. Magnitude: Full AUC on
ZC = 0.957 vs. BFree = 0.675 (28 pp inflation).
Mechanism 2 (Level 3 MBO contemporaneous leakage): Rolling aggregation windows com-
puted at time t include the current second. Without shift(1), features at time t contain same-second
information. Correction: shift(1) on all rolling features. Magnitude: mean L3 gain pre-fix = +0.035
AUC, post-fix = +0.0015 AUC — a 23-fold reduction (mean-to-mean across all 8 instruments).
The single-instrument maximum (NQ) shows pre-fix gain of +0.10 declining to +0.018 post-fix, a
5.6-fold reduction.
Mechanism 3 (Level 2 market structure reflection): End-of-second book state incorporates
all intra-second orders and trades. Without lagging, the model has indirect access to concurrent
trading activity. Correction: universal one-second lag on all book state features. M3 is corrected
simultaneously with M2 through the universal shift(1); its individual contribution is not separately
identifiable.
Mechanism 4 (Level 1 trade flow contamination):Full-second aggregated signed trade volume
includes trades that caused the price change being predicted. Correction: universal one-second lag.
This mechanism is the primary source of the gap between contemporaneous OFI studies (Cont et
al., 2014, report large effects) and lagged OFI studies (our mean |r| = 0.019). M4 is corrected
simultaneously with M2 and M3.
4.5 Significance Criteria and Multiple Testing
Family-wise error rate α = 0.05 with Bonferroni correction across 1,104 unique tests (46 cells × 4
horizons × 2 feature sets × 3 designs). Per-test threshold: α/1,104 ≈ 4.53 × 10−5.
Each model undergoes five shuffle tests (training labels randomly permuted with seeds 1000-
1004). The shuffle-based significance threshold is derived as follows: under the null hypothesis
that the model has learned no genuine signal, test AUC follows a distribution centered at 0.50. We
require real AUC to exceed shuffle mean plus k standard deviations, where k satisfies P(Z > k) =
α/1,104 under a Gaussian approximation, yielding k ≈ 4.08. Shuffle baselines are computed for
tree-based and logistic regression architectures; deep learning architectures do not generate shuffle
baselines due to computational cost. Across the 1,656 non-DL isolation runs with shuffle baselines
(3 architectures × 46 cells × 4 horizons × 3 designs, BFree feature set), the mean shuffle AUC is
0.502 and the maximum is 0.552. The 95th percentile of shuffle AUC is 0.508, confirming that the
shuffle distribution is tightly concentrated near 0.50 (Figure 5).
Architecture selection bias. For each cell, we report the best-architecture result (highest test
AUC across six architectures). This introduces an upward selection bias: the best of six draws from
the AUC distribution is expected to exceed the median. We do not formally correct for this in the
Bonferroni threshold, meaning our significance criterion is slightly lenient. However, the shuffle test
provides a model-specific control: each architecture’s shuffle AUC is computed independently, so the
comparison between a model’s real AUC and its own shuffle distribution accounts for architecture-
specific baseline inflation. The practical impact is small because the best-to-second-best architecture
gap is typically < 0.01 (median across 46 cells), and the signal-to-noise ratio in T1-T3 cells (edge >
13

0.04) substantially exceeds this selection effect.

5.4 The NQ Anomaly: Highest HFT, Lowest Signal
NQ (E-mini Nasdaq) is uniformly weak across all seven session buckets: median edge ranges from

consistency across sessions is itself informative — unlike ES, which shows an approximately 7 ×
overnight-to-RTH edge ratio, NQ maintains high HFT participation even during overnight hours,
producing uniformly flat predictability.
5.5 Architecture and Horizon Patterns
Transformer produces the best cell-level result in 26/46 cells, CNN in 12, LSTM in 3, XGBoost in
3, LogReg in 2, LightGBM in 0. However, the margin between best and second-best architecture
within each cell is typically small: median gap < 0.01, with 80% of cells showing gaps below 0.015.
At these margins, the architecture “winner” in any given cell may be determined by noise rather than
genuine architectural advantage. An earlier full-session analysis showed tree models dominating DL
— a reversal that occurred because session-bucketed data reduces sample size per cell, potentially
giving DL models a relative advantage on smaller datasets with more structured temporal patterns.
We do not interpret the architecture rankings as evidence of categorical DL superiority; under a
no-tuning protocol, the differences are likely within the range of random variation.

5.7 Case Study: ZC (Corn) — The K-Algorithm Bounce Mechanism in Action
ZC is the strongest-signal instrument in the study and the clearest window into how matching al-
gorithm design creates predictable price paths. All five ZC buckets achieve T1 status, with median
edges ranging from +0.272 (open) to +0.361 (overnight). The cross-design replication is exception-
ally tight: ZC/afternoon varies by only 0.005 across three designs, the tightest replication in the
entire 46-cell dataset.
The mechanism is observable directly in the order book data. Under K-Algorithm matching,
the pro-rata allocation component incentivizes participants to maintain large resting orders at the
best bid and ask — a trader resting 100 contracts captures a proportionally larger fill share than one
resting 10 contracts. This concentrated depth creates a characteristic pattern when an aggressive
market order arrives: the aggressor consumes a portion of the resting depth, moving the mid-price
by one tick in the aggressor’s direction. The remaining resting depth — still substantial because the
pro-rata incentive keeps it large — immediately reasserts the original price level. The result is a
one-tick bounce with 70% probability at the one-second horizon.
This bounce is what the model learns to predict. At the 5-second horizon, the model’s AUC of
0.873 reflects its ability to identify which snapshots precede a bounce (predictable) versus which
precede a genuine directional move (less predictable). The BFree feature set, which removes price-
lagged returns, still achieves AUC of 0.675 — indicating that order book state features (depth im-
balance, spread, cancel-to-trade ratio) carry substantial predictive content even without the return
autocorrelation that directly encodes bounce.

The practical irony is that ZC’s statistical strength is precisely what makes it economically un-
reachable. The pro-rata allocation that creates the bounce also ensures that a retail trader’s 1-contract

FIFO/pro-rata split, a 1-lot order arriving first gets the 40% FIFO slice, but if 200 contracts are rest-
ing, the 60% pro-rata portion allocates approximately 0.3 contracts (rounded to zero) to the 1-lot
participant. The signal is real but the execution mechanics prevent its capture.

5.8 Case Study: NQ (Nasdaq) — Maximum HFT, Minimum Signal
NQ is the anti-ZC: the instrument with the most data (4.13 billion MBO events, 60% of the total), the

The uniformity across sessions is the key finding. ES shows an approximately 7 × overnight-to-
RTH ratio. NQ shows only ~1.2 ×. This means NQ’s HFT ecosystem does not substantially thin
out during overnight hours — the algorithmic market makers that patrol NQ’s order book during US
regular trading hours continue to operate during Asian and European sessions, maintaining the same
level of informational efficiency. Whatever residual inefficiency exists in ES overnight (where some

market makers pull quotes) does not exist in NQ overnight.

existed because of a fundamental information advantage (e.g., fewer informed participants during
overnight), it should appear in NQ overnight as well — NQ and ES are economically linked through

NQ overnight generates the highest dollar profit per trade ($45) at the market-maker tier. NQ’s $5

allocation), and the notional contract value (~$420K for NQ versus ~$34K for ZC) means each filled
trade captures more economic value. The instrument with the weakest signal is the most valuable to
trade — a finding that inverts the naive assumption that stronger signals produce more profit.

6. Economic Exploitability
6.1 Execution Scenarios
We evaluate exploitability under three execution scenarios for each of the 46 cells, reflecting the
range of market access from retail to professional.
Scenario A (Full aggressor): Market order entry and exit. The trader crosses the full spread on
both sides and pays exchange fees. Cost per round-turn = full spread + 2 × per-side fee. This is the
worst-case execution representing a retail trader using market orders.
Scenario B (Passive entry, aggressive exit): Limit order entry at mid-price (no spread cost on
entry, but only 50% of signals result in a fill before the move occurs), market order exit. Cost per
trade = half spread + 2 × per-side fee. Expected profit is multiplied by the fill rate: signals that don’t
get filled contribute zero to P&L but also zero cost.
Scenario C (Full passive): Passive entry and exit on limit orders. The trader earns the spread
rather than paying it. Fill rate depends on matching algorithm: 100% FIFO priority for the six
FIFO instruments (assuming time priority at an uncrowded level), 40% FIFO allocation for ZC/ZS
under K-Algorithm. For K-Algorithm instruments, the remaining 60% pro-rata allocation yields
fill probability of approximately (trader_size / total_resting_size) — typically 1/200 to 1/500 for a
1-contract retail order.

The more informative analysis isolates the per-side fee effect from the spread-direction effect.
Holding spread treatment constant (always paying), no cell achieves profitability at any fee level
tested — the edges are simply too small relative to one-tick spread costs. Holding fees constant at
zero but varying spread treatment (paying vs earning), the transition to profitability occurs for all

— a result that has implications for exchange design (spread-narrowing mechanisms would shift the

Worked example: ZC/overnight under Scenario A (retail). ZC overnight has median edge
+0.361, implying directional accuracy a = 0.50 + 0.361 = 0.861. ZC tick value = $12.50. Mean
absolute 5-second mid-price move on ZC overnight ≈ 0.15 ticks ($1.88). Round-turn retail cost = 1
tick spread × $12.50 + 2 × $2.50 fees = $17.50.
Expected profit per trade = a × $1.88 − (1−a) × $1.88 − $17.50 = 0.861 × $1.88 − 0.139 ×
$1.88 − $17.50 = $1.62 − $0.26 − $17.50 = −$16.14.
Even with 86% accuracy, the expected 5-second move ($1.88) is dwarfed by the round-turn cost
($17.50). The signal would need to predict moves of at least $12.14 (approximately 1 full tick) to
break even at retail — a magnitude that occurs in less than 5% of 5-second windows on ZC overnight.
Under Scenario C (market maker). Same accuracy (86.1%). Cost = 2 × $0.10 = $0.20 (fees
only, spread earned not paid). Revenue per filled trade ≈ 1 tick earned ($12.50) × fill rate (40%
FIFO allocation on K-Algorithm) = $5.00 per attempt. Net per attempt = 0.40 × (0.861 × $12.50 −
0.139 × $12.50 − $0.20) = 0.40 × ($10.76 − $1.74 − $0.20) = 0.40 × $8.82 = +$3.53.
6.3 The NQ Dollar-Value Paradox
A finding with direct implications for market design: the instruments with the strongest statistical
signal are not the most economically valuable.
ZC/overnight has the strongest edge (+0.361, AUC 0.873) but generates modest dollar profit per
trade at the MM tier because ZC’s pro-rata fill allocation limits fill probability. NQ/asia_overnight
has the weakest statistical signal among T4 cells (+0.018, AUC 0.524) but generates the highest
dollar profit per trade ($45) because NQ has FIFO matching (100% fill priority), tight 1-tick spreads
($5), and the highest notional contract value (~$420K).
This divergence between statistical and economic ranking is a direct consequence of matching
algorithm design: K-Algorithm creates strong statistical patterns but limits the ability to capture
them through pro-rata allocation, while FIFO creates weaker patterns but allows complete capture
through time priority.
6.4 Cross-Cell Correlations
The correlation between edge and Scenario C surplus is ρ = +0.89 (p < 10 −15), confirming that
stronger signals produce proportionally larger profits at the MM tier. Additional correlations from

7.2 Bounce Rate Mediation
The matching algorithm effect operates through the bounce rate mechanism. The correlation be-
tween bounce rate and edge-over-chance is ρ = +0.94 (p < 10−15), indicating that bounce rate effec-
tively mediates the matching algorithm → predictability relationship.
K-Algorithm matching incentivizes large resting orders at the top of book to capture pro-rata
allocation. When incoming market orders consume this concentrated depth, the price briefly moves
in the aggressor’s direction, then reverts as the remaining depth reasserts. This mechanical bounce
is deterministic given the order book state, producing highly predictable short-horizon price paths.
Our analysis documents the bounce rate hierarchy across the two matching algorithm families
present in our sample: K-Algorithm instruments (ZC, ZS) exhibit mean bounce rate of 0.70 versus
0.56 for FIFO instruments (ES, NQ, CL, GC, HE, LE). Within K-Algorithm instruments, overnight
ZC bounce rate (0.71) is nearly identical to RTH ZC bounce rate (0.69), indicating that the bounce
mechanism is structural to the matching algorithm rather than varying with participation intensity.
An earlier analysis classified HE and LE as “Pro Rata” instruments with a 15% bounce rate; subse-
quent verification against CME Globex product specifications confirmed that HE and LE use FIFO
matching for outright futures, and their low bounce rates reflect thin-market dynamics rather than a
distinct matching algorithm.

8. Academic Signal Comparison
8.1 Signal Construction
We implement three academic microstructure signals per cell plus one redundant baseline fea-
ture: OFI (Cont et al., 2014, top-of-book with 10s rolling aggregation), VPIN (Easley et al., 2012,
50-bucket rolling windows), OFA (signed trade flow autocorrelation at 5s lags), and DAR (depth
asymmetry ratio). DAR is algebraically identical to l1_imbalance — both compute (bid_size_1 −
ask_size_1) / (bid_size_1 + ask_size_1) — which is already in the BFree baseline. In the simple
logistic regression, DAR provides predictive content because the other three signals do not encode
depth asymmetry. In the ML pipeline, DAR adds zero marginal ∆AUC (confirmed across all 43
testable cells) because LightGBM already extracts the same information from l1_imbalance. The
simple-vs-ML capture ratio therefore reflects the contribution of three genuinely new signals (OFI,
VPIN, OFA) plus the depth asymmetry feature that both approaches share.

8.3 The OFI Contemporaneous vs. Predictive Distinction
An earlier full-session analysis established a critical finding: Cont et al.’s (2014) OFI is a contempo-
raneous measure (OFI at time t correlates with returns at time t), not a predictive one. After imposing
the mandatory one-second causal lag, snapshot OFI correlation collapses from the published values
to mean |r| = 0.019 across eight instruments — economically negligible. Trade OFI retains a small
non-linear signal on ES (r = 0.019 linear, but +0.019 AUC via tree-based ML), suggesting OFI’s
predictive content is captured through interaction terms rather than linear correlation.
The per-bucket analysis adds granularity: the OFI signal, such as it is, concentrates in overnight
sessions on liquid FIFO instruments. During RTH sessions, even the non-linear OFI component
vanishes. This is consistent with the interpretation that OFI captures residual information that HFT
market makers have not yet fully incorporated — which is more abundant during overnight hours.
VPIN produces a complete null across all instruments, sessions, and horizons (max | ∆AUC| <
0.003), consistent with Andersen and Bondarenko’s (2014) critique.

9. Robustness Checks
9.1 Alternative Label Definitions
We test 320 alternative label configurations spanning five label types to ensure findings are not
specific to binary direction prediction.
Label L1 (binary direction) is the primary label. Labels L2a-L2c apply magnitude filters (1, 2,
3 ticks minimum). Label L3 is a five-class signed magnitude label. Labels L4a-L4c test directional
persistence (3, 5, 10 consecutive seconds). Labels L5a-L5b implement the triple barrier method
(1-tick and 2-tick barriers).
The persistence labels (L4) are theoretically most important because they are by construction
bounce-free: bounce reverses within 1-2 seconds and cannot generate 3-second persistence labels.
L4a shows genuine signal in 24/32 instrument-horizon configurations — real directional content
that persists beyond bounce. However, this signal remains insufficient to clear transaction costs. The
economic conclusion is robust across all label definitions.
9.2 Data Depth Ablation
192 ablation experiments test the marginal value of data tiers. After correcting for the contempora-
neous leakage mechanism (Section 4.4), Level 3 MBO features add only +0.0015 AUC on average
beyond Level 2. The pre-fix apparent gain of +0.046 demonstrates the magnitude of Mechanism 2
distortion. This finding has practical implications: researchers paying premium prices for MBO data
should not expect marginal predictive value at one-second resolution under correct causal construc-
tion.
9.3 Pooled Analysis
3,840 pooled runs (all buckets combined per instrument with bucket-indicator features) produce
median edge approximately equivalent to isolation (delta +0.003). Grain instruments show mild
pooling benefit; ES/CL show isolation advantage. This confirms predictability is session-specific:
adding time-of-day context does not unlock signal beyond what session-specialized models capture.
9.4 Leakage Re-Verification (A9)
Feature ablation on the 17 T1/T2 cells (582 ablation runs) confirms: the maximum single-feature sig-
nal contribution is 2.16%, with vwap_distance appearing most frequently in cells’ top-5 (15/17 cells)
but never exceeding ~2%. Signal is distributed across multiple features — principally vwap_distance,
net_delta_1s, and l1_imbalance — with no single feature dominating. This rules out the possibility
that the documented predictability is a feature-construction artifact.

9.5 Cross-Instrument Transfer
Models trained on one instrument fail to predict returns on correlated instruments: ZC →ZS cross-
instrument AUC = 0.512 (vs. ZS self = 0.674), ES →NQ cross AUC = 0.502 (vs. NQ self = 0.598).
Microstructure signals encode instrument-specific order book dynamics, not shared fundamental
information. This is consistent with the session-specific finding: the signal reflects local book struc-
ture, not market-wide information.
10. Limitations and Future Research
10.1 Limitations
Single calendar year and within-sample designs. All results derive from January-December 2025.
The three holdout designs partition the same underlying distribution: Designs A and B use identical
structure (6 blocks, 10 days each, same embargo) with different random seeds, varying only which
blocks are selected. Design C (walk-forward) is the only design that meaningfully varies the tempo-
ral structure, and its early folds train on as few as 4 months. The cross-design Spearman of 0.97+ is
therefore largely a measure of within-sample stability — the signal is not an artifact of a particular
holdout draw — rather than a test of temporal robustness or out-of-sample generalization. January
2026 data was excluded per the pre-registration and provides a natural forward test that we leave for
future work.
Two K-Algorithm instruments. The matching algorithm comparison is based on 2 instru-
ments (ZC, ZS) versus 6 FIFO instruments. With N=2, the K-Algorithm effect cannot be separated
from grain-specific confounds (commercial hedging patterns, seasonal crop dynamics, lower abso-
lute HFT participation). The within-FIFO C/T-vs-edge correlation ( ρ = −0.61) provides a cleaner
single-mechanism test because it operates across 6 instruments with 36 cells.
HE/LE matching algorithm clarification. An earlier version of this analysis classified HE
and LE as “Pro Rata” instruments. Verification against CME Globex product specifications con-
firmed that HE and LE outright futures use FIFO matching. The earlier three-category compari-
son (FIFO/K-Algorithm/Pro Rata) was incorrect; the correct two-category comparison is reported
throughout this paper.
Conservative cost model. The trading simulation assumes stylized fill rates and one trade per 5-
minute window. A realistic market simulator with queue position modeling, partial fills, and adverse
selection would produce more precise exploitability estimates.
Agricultural seasonality. ZC and ZS exhibit seasonal microstructure variation. The walk-
forward Design C places harvest season in later test folds, creating potential distribution shift. We
document this shift but do not conduct an alternative seasonal split.
Fixed DL hyperparameters. Deep learning models use fixed hyperparameters across all cells
to prevent ex-post optimization. This is methodologically honest but structurally disadvantages DL
relative to tree models, which are less sensitive to hyperparameter choice. The DL architecture
results should be interpreted under this constraint.

DL convergence failures. Twenty DL runs (all on GC pooled configurations) failed due to GPU
memory constraints when the GC pooled dataset exceeded available VRAM during sequence con-
struction. These runs were excluded from the analysis — they are not re-run and not imputed. The
exclusion affects only the GC pooled DL rows; all GC isolation runs and all GC pooled tree/LogReg
runs completed successfully. The 20 excluded runs represent 0.5% of pooled runs (20 of 3,840) and

10.2 Future Research
Longer horizons. Extending from 5-60 seconds to 1-60 minutes would determine whether the signal
transforms or decays at execution-relevant timescales. This is the most natural follow-up paper.
Cross-instrument signal propagation. Whether ZC microstructure signals predict ZS price
movements at lagged horizons — and whether any propagation lag is sufficient for cross-instrument
execution — is an open question with direct practical relevance.
SHAP feature interpretation. While Section 8 demonstrates that depth asymmetry captures
the majority of ML signal, a full SHAP analysis would reveal whether the non-linear component has
coherent economic interpretation.
Natural experiments. CME periodically modifies matching algorithm parameters. Such modifi-
cations provide natural experiments for testing the causal effect of algorithm design on predictability.
Regime conditioning. Whether signal strength varies with USDA report days, FOMC an-
nouncements, roll periods, or seasonal crop cycles would distinguish structural from event-driven
predictability.
11. Conclusion
This paper documents a precise, replicable, and economically interpretable relationship between
market structure and short-horizon price predictability across eight CME futures instruments. Using
6.83 billion Market-by-Order events and a pre-registered methodology producing 10,464 model fits,
we establish three findings.
First, the pre-registered null was upheld: no cell achieves economic exploitability. However,
directional predictability at 5-60 second horizons is ubiquitous, replicating across three holdout de-
signs with rank correlation exceeding 0.97.
Second, predictability magnitude is associated with matching algorithm design (K-Algorithm in-
struments show 8.3× FIFO predictability, consistent with bounce rate mediation atρ = 0.94, though
based on only two K-Algorithm instruments) and with HFT participation intensity (overnight 4.0 ×
RTH, C/T ratio ρ = −0.65 across 46 cells).
Third, the exploitability gap is dominated by the spread-direction arithmetic: earning versus
paying the spread determines profitability more than any signal or fee parameter. No cell profits at
retail costs; all cells profit when spreads are earned.
Simple academic signals capture a median 89.5% of the ML edge, indicating that the documented
predictability reflects known order book mechanics operating at varying intensities across market

structure regimes. ML adds genuine value only on high-volume FIFO overnight cells where non-
linear interactions matter.
These results reframe market efficiency from a binary property to a continuous function of market
access costs, with matching algorithm design as a previously unquantified determinant. For exchange
operators, the finding that algorithm choice directly affects the statistical properties of price paths
informs the policy debate around pro-rata versus FIFO priority. For the academic literature, the pre-
registered three-design methodology provides a template for rigorous signal validation in an era of
replication concerns.
